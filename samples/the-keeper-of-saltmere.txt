For my grandmother, who kept a light burning.



Chapter One: The Lamp

The lamp at Harrow's Point had not gone dark in forty-one years, and Mara Quinn did not intend to be the one who let it.

She climbed the iron stairs with the oil can knocking against her knee, counting the steps the way her father had taught her when she was small enough to ride on his shoulders. One hundred and twelve. At the top the lantern room smelled of brass polish and salt, and the great lens threw her reflection back at her in a hundred broken pieces. Mara was seventeen years old, and tonight, for the first time, the light was hers alone.

Below, the village of Saltmere huddled against the cliff like something that had been washed up and forgotten. Smoke rose from the chimneys. A dog barked twice and stopped. Out past the breakwater the sea was the colour of slate, and the wind had begun to sing in the rigging of the boats.

Her father, Elias Quinn, had gone to the mainland on the morning ferry. "Two days," he had said, pressing the keys into her palm. "Keep it trimmed, keep it lit, and keep your brother out of the the oil store." He had kissed her forehead and walked down the jetty without looking back, which was how she knew he was worried.

Mara's green eyes stung as she lit the wick. The flame caught, steadied, and then the lens took it and made it enormous, a white blade sweeping out across the water. She let out a breath she didn't know she was holding.

"You could of waited for me," said Tobias from the doorway. He was eleven and permanently out of breath, his hair standing up as if he had been struck by lightning. "Mrs. Hale says a storm is coming. She says she can feel it in her knees."

"Mrs. Hale feels everything in her knees."

"She says the last time her knees were this bad, the Mary Ellen went down with all hands." Tobias came to stand beside her, pressing his nose to the glass. "Do you think Father will come back on monday?"

"He said two days."

"That isn't an answer."

It wasn't. Mara watched the beam travel out and come back, out and back, and tried not to think about the letter she had found in her father's coat pocket that morning, folded small, with a seal she did not recognise.

Chapter Two: Mrs. Hale

Mrs. Hale lived in the last cottage before the lighthouse path, and she had opinions about everyone in Saltmere, most of them correct.

"Your father's a fool," she said the next morning, pouring tea so strong it was nearly black. "Leaving two children alone with a storm coming. I told him so."

"I'm not a child," said Mara.

"You're seventeen. That's a child with longer legs." Mrs. Hale pushed a plate of oatcakes across the table. Her kitchen was crowded with jars and bundles of drying herbs, and a grey cat watched them from the top of the dresser with undisguised contempt. "Eat. You look like a heron."

Mara ate. Tobias had already eaten three oatcakes and was working on a fourth, crumbs scattered down his jumper. Mrs. Hale watched him with the fond exasperation she reserved for small boys and stray animals.

"Mrs. Hale," Mara said carefully, "did my father say why he was going to the mainland?"

The old woman's hands went still on the teapot. It was only for a moment, but Mara saw it. "Business," Mrs. Hale said. "Lighthouse business. The Board sends for the keepers now and then."

"He took the brass box."

"Did he."

"The one from under the stairs. The one he says is full of old logbooks."

Mrs. Hale set the teapot down with great care. "Then I expect he needed his logbooks," she said, and began, very deliberately, to talk about the price of herring.

Walking back up the path, Tobias kicked a stone ahead of him. "She knows something."

"Everyone in Saltmere knows something," Mara said. "That's the problem with Saltmere." But she was thinking of the way Mrs. Hale's hands had stopped, and of the letter, and of the seal pressed into the red wax: a ship inside a circle, and beneath it, a single word she had not been able to read.

The wind had turned. It came now from the north-east, cold and steady, and the gulls had gone inland.

Chapter Three: The Letter

That night Mara took the letter out again and spread it on the table in the keeper's room. She had told herself she would not read it. She had told herself that a great many times, and then she read it anyway, by the light of the oil lamp, while the real lamp turned and turned above her head.

It was not long. Most of it was written in a cramped, slanting hand she did not know, and some of the words had run where the paper had got wet.

Elias, it said. They have found the Corrigan papers. If the Board learns what happened in the winter of the wreck, they will take the light from you and worse. Bring the box. Tell no one, not even the children. Especially not the girl, she has her mother's eyes and her mother's stubbornness. Come alone. — R.

Mara read it three times. Her heart skipped a beat each time she reached the word wreck.

Her mother had died when Mara was four. That was all she knew, because that was all anyone in Saltmere would tell her. Her mother had died, and her father had kept the light, and they did not talk about it. Not ever.

She looked up and caught her reflection in the dark window: a pale, narrow face, Mara's blue eyes wide and frightened, her hair coming loose from its plait. She looked, she thought, like someone in a story who was about to make a very bad decision.

"What are you reading?" Tobias was on the stairs in his nightshirt, rubbing his eyes.

"Nothing. Go to bed."

"It's not nothing. Your doing the face."

"What face?"

"The face you did when you broke Father's barometer and said it was the wind."

She folded the letter and put it in her pocket. "It was the wind," she said. "Go to bed, Tobias."

He went, grumbling. Mara sat alone with the turning light and the sound of the sea, which had grown louder while she wasn't listening, and she made the very bad decision anyway. Tomorrow she would find out who R. was.

Chapter Four: The Harbourmaster

The harbourmaster's office stood at the end of the stone quay, a squat building with a slate roof and a weathervane shaped like a cod. Rufus Penhallow had been harbourmaster for longer than Mara had been alive, and he had the face of a man who had been personally disappointed by the sea on many occasions.

"Your father's not here," he said, before she had even shut the door.

"I know. I wanted to ask you something."

"Ask it quick. There's weather coming and I've boats to see to." He definately did not look up from the ledger in front of him. His handwriting, she noticed, sloped hard to the right.

Mara took a breath. "What happened in the winter of the wreck?"

Penhallow's pen stopped. Very slowly, he looked up at her, and she saw something move behind his eyes, something that might have been fear and might have been grief.

"Who told you to ask me that?"

"Nobody."

"Then nobody can tell you the answer." He put the pen down. "Go home, Mara. Keep the light. That's all your father would want you to do."

"Did you write to him? Are you R.?"

For a long moment he didn't answer. Outside, a halyard clanged against a mast, over and over, like a bell. Then Penhallow stood up, walked to the window, and stood with his back to her.

"In order to understand what happened," he said at last, "you'd need to understand your mother. And I'm not the one who should tell you about her."

"Then who is?"

He turned around. He looked, suddenly, much older. "Ask your father," he said. "When he comes back. If he comes back."

She walked home along the cliff with the wind shoving at her back. Below, the sea had begun to break white over the outer rocks, and the fishing boats were being hauled up the slipway one by one. A shiver ran down her spine that had nothing to do with the cold.

Chapter Five: The Storm

The storm came in the night, exactly as Mrs. Hale's knees had promised.

Mara woke to the whole tower shuddering. Rain was hitting the windows sideways, a hard, gravelly sound, and under it was a deeper noise, a roar that she felt in her teeth. She was out of bed and on the stairs before she was properly awake.

The lamp was still burning. She stood in the lantern room with her hands pressed against the cold glass and watched the beam swing out into a world that had turned entirely to water. Waves were breaking over the breakwater. Spray was reaching the foot of the cliff, a hundred feet below her.

"Mara!" Tobias was shouting from the bottom of the stairs. "Mara, there's a light! On the water!"

She saw it then: a small yellow glow, rising and falling, far too close to the Teeth, the line of black rocks that had given Harrow's Point its reputation and its lighthouse. A boat. Someone was out there.

Mara was sixteen years old and she had never been so frightened in her life. She knew what she was supposed to do. Keep the light. That was all. Keep the light, and let the lifeboat crew at Saltmere do the rest.

But the lifeboat crew would not see the boat from the village. Nobody would see it but her.

She grabbed the signal lamp from its hook and began to flash the warning, the way her father had shown her: long, short, long. Turn away. Turn away. The yellow light on the water swung, hesitated, and began, very slowly, to turn.

"It's working," Tobias breathed beside her. "Mara, it's working."

She kept flashing until her arm ached and her fingers went numb, until the yellow light had cleared the Teeth and was crawling toward the harbour mouth. Then she sat down hard on the iron floor and found that she was crying, and that Tobias was holding her hand.

"Who would be out in this?" he whispered.

Mara didn't answer. She was thinking of the morning ferry, and of the brass box, and of her father, who had said two days.

Chapter Six: What the Sea Returned

The boat was the Corrigan's Pride, and it was not her father who came off it.

By the time the storm blew itself out, the whole of Saltmere had come down to the harbour to see. The boat lay against the quay with its mast broken and its paint stripped back to bare wood. Two men from the lifeboat crew helped a figure up the ladder: a woman, wrapped in a borrowed oilskin, her grey hair plastered to her skull.

Mrs. Hale, standing beside Mara, made a small sound, as if someone had struck her.

"Who is she?" Mara asked.

Mrs. Hale did not answer. She was staring at the woman on the quay, and her face had gone the colour of ash.

The woman looked up. Her eyes travelled over the crowd, over the fishermen and the wives and the children, and came to rest on Mara. Something in her face broke open.

"Rosalind," whispered Mrs. Hale. "Dear God. Rosalind Corrigan."

"Who is Rosalind Corrigan?" Tobais demanded, tugging at Mrs. Hale's sleeve. "Mrs. Hale? Who is she?"

But Mara already knew. R. The cramped, slanting hand. The ship inside a circle. She knew it the way you know a word you have never read but have always somehow understood.

The woman on the quay took a step toward her, and then another, and the crowd parted to let her through.

"You have her eyes," Rosalind Corrigan said. Her voice was hoarse from salt and shouting. "You have your mother's eyes exactly."

"My mother is dead," said Mara.

"No," said Rosalind Corrigan. "No, child. That's what we told them. That's what we had to tell everyone."

Chapter Eight: The Brass Box

They sat in Mrs. Hale's kitchen with the door bolted and the curtains drawn, and Rosalind Corrigan told them about the winter of the wreck.

"Your mother and I were sisters," she said. She held her teacup in both hands, as if it was the only warm thing left in the world. "Annie was the younger. Cleverer than me. Braver. She married your father against everyone's wishes, and they kept the light together."

Tobias had gone very quiet. Mara could feel him beside her, barely breathing.

"Fourteen years ago a ship went onto the Teeth," Rosalind went on. "A ship called the Mary Ellen. The light was out that night. Not for long. Ten minutes, perhaps. But ten minutes was enough." She closed her eyes. "Nineteen men drowned."

"The light never goes out," Mara said. "Forty-one years—"

"That's the story. That's the story your father told the Board, and Rufus Penhallow swore to it in the ledger, and every soul in Saltmere kept their mouths shut because the Corrigan family owned the ship and the Corrigan family wanted it buried." Rosalind's mouth twisted. "Annie wouldn't keep quiet. She was going to tell the truth. So she went away. We told everyone she had drowned. It was the only way to keep her safe from my own father."

"Where is she now?" Mara's voice did not sound like her own.

"On the mainland. Alive. Ill, now, and asking for her children." Rosalind looked at her. "Your father has the box. The real logbook from that night is inside it, the one that shows the truth. He's taking it to the Board. He means to confess."

"They'll take the light from him," Mrs. Hale said quietly.

"Yes."

"And worse."

"Yes."

Mara stood up. She did not decide to; her legs simply did it for her. "Then we have to go to the mainland," she said. "Tonight."

"There's no ferry until Thursday," said Mrs. Hale.

"Then we'll take a boat."

Rosalind Corrigan looked at Mara for a long moment, and then, unexpectedly, she smiled. It transformed her whole face. "There," she said softly. "There's Annie."

Chapter Nine: Crossing

They took the Corrigan's Pride, patched and pumped and rigged with a borrowed mast, because it was the only boat in Saltmere whose owner was not afraid of the Corrigan family.

Rufus Penhallow came down to the slipway at dawn to see them off. He stood with his hands in his pockets and his collar turned up, and for a long time he didn't say anything at all.

"I wrote it in the ledger," he said finally. "What your father told me to write. I've regretted it every day for fourteen years."

"Then help us," said Mara.

He looked at her. Then he pulled off his coat, climbed down into the boat, and took the tiller without another word.

The crossing took most of the day. The sea was still lumpy after the storm, a long grey swell that lifted the boat and set it down again, lifted and set down, until Tobias was sick over the side and lay with his head in Mara's lap, groaning. Rosalind sat in the bow and watched the horizon. Penhallow steered and said nothing.

Mara thought about her mother. She tried to remember her face and found that she could not; there was only a voice, singing, and the smell of oil and brass, and a pair of hands lifting her up to see the light. She had built her whole life around a grave that was empty. She did not know yet whether she was angry or glad. She suspected she was both, and that she would be both for a very long time.

Late in the afternoon the mainland rose out of the haze: a grey smudge, then cliffs, then the spires and chimneys of Port Ardent. Penhallow brought them into the harbour as the lamps were being lit along the front.

"The Board sits in the Customs House," he said. "Tomorrow at ten. If your father's here, that's where he'll be."

"And my mother?"

Rosalind took her hand. Her fingers were cold and rough and very strong. "Tonight," she said. "I'll take you to her tonight."

Chapter Ten: The Light

Annie Quinn was smaller than Mara had imagined, and thinner, and her hair had gone white at the temples. She lay propped on pillows in a narrow room above a chandler's shop, and when Mara came through the door she put out both her hands and said Mara's name as if she had been saying it every day for fourteen years.

Perhaps she had.

Mara did not remember crossing the room. She remembered only the hands, the same hands that had lifted her up to see the light, and the voice, and the smell of oil and brass that was somehow still there after all this time.

Her father came the next morning. He stood in the doorway with the brass box under his arm, and he looked at his wife and his children, and his face did something Mara had never seen it do before.

"I was going to tell them," Elias Quinn said. "At ten o'clock. All of it."

"I know," said Annie. "Rosalind told me."

"They'll take the light."

"Let them," said Annie. "It was never the light that mattered, Elias. It was the truth. We kept the light for fourteen years and let the truth go dark."

At ten o'clock they went to the Customs House together, all of them: Elias with the box, and Rosalind, and Rufus Penhallow, who had regretted it every day for fourteen years, and Annie in a borrowed wheelchair with Tobias pushing it, and Mara walking beside her, holding her hand.

What the Board decided is another story. What matters is that the truth was told, and the names of nineteen drowned men were read aloud in a public room for the first time, and that afterwards, on the steps outside, Mara Quinn looked out across the water toward Saltmere and saw, very faintly in the gathering dusk, the light at Harrow's Point still burning.

Someone had kept it lit.

She never did find out who.
